## Why speed matters

If you typed your password into a fake page, assume the attacker has it. Attackers often try stolen passwords within minutes, and they try them on other services too. The steps below are most effective in the first hour, so do them now and report afterwards, or ask someone to report while you change passwords.

## Step 1: change the password

From a device you trust, go to the real website by typing the address yourself (not through any link in the message) and change the password. Use a new, long password that you have never used anywhere else. If you can no longer sign in because the password has already been changed, go straight to account recovery and contact the organisation's support.

## Step 2: change it everywhere it was reused

If you used the same password, or a very similar one, on any other account, change those too. Start with your email account, because whoever controls your email can reset almost everything else. A password manager makes it practical to have a different password for every account.

## Step 3: sign out other sessions

Most services let you sign out of all devices or end all active sessions from the security settings. Do this after changing the password, so that an attacker who already signed in is thrown out.

## Step 4: check multi-factor authentication

If MFA was not turned on, turn it on now. If it was on, check that the registered phone numbers, authenticator apps and recovery options are all yours and that nothing new was added. If you also gave away an MFA code or approved a prompt, follow the MFA guidance as well, because the attacker may already be inside the account.

## Step 5: look for changes the attacker may have made

In an email account, check for new forwarding rules, filters that delete or move messages, changed recovery email or phone number, new connected apps, and sent messages you did not write. Attackers set these up to keep access and to hide warning emails from you.

## Step 6: report it

Report what happened to the organisation that owns the account, and for a work or college account, to its IT helpdesk, even after you have changed the password. Say when it happened, what you typed into the page, and whether you used the password anywhere else. Reporting quickly lets them check for suspicious sign-ins and protect other users who received the same message.
