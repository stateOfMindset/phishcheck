## Text messages (smishing)

Phishing by SMS or messaging apps often impersonates delivery companies, banks, government services or the college, with a short urgent message and a link: a parcel is held, a fee is unpaid, an account is locked. Links in text messages are harder to inspect on a phone, so the safest habit is not to open them at all and to check through the official app or website instead. Never reply with a code you received.

## Phone calls (vishing)

A caller may claim to be from IT support, the bank or the police, and may already know some details about you to sound convincing. Common goals are getting you to read out an MFA code, approve a sign-in prompt, install remote-access software, or move money. Hang up and call back using a number you look up yourself. Real support staff will never need your password or a one-time code.

## QR codes (quishing)

QR codes hide the address they lead to, and attackers put them in emails, on posters or stickers placed over real codes (for example on parking machines or notice boards). A QR code in an email that asks you to sign in or "re-verify" your account is a strong warning sign, partly because it moves you from a protected work computer to a personal phone. Before opening a scanned link, read the address your phone shows; if it is not the organisation's real domain, do not continue.

## The same rules apply

Whatever the channel, the defences are the same: be wary of urgency, never share passwords or codes, verify through a channel you trust, and report what you received. If you already acted on the message, follow the same steps as for an email: the guidance for clicked links, entered passwords and MFA codes applies equally.
