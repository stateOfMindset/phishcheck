## What phishing is

Phishing is a message that pretends to come from someone you trust (your college, a bank, a delivery company, a colleague) so that you click a link, open a file, or hand over information such as a password, an MFA code or payment details. It arrives most often by email, but also by SMS, WhatsApp, social media and phone calls. Most phishing does not need any technical trick: it works by making you act quickly before you think.

## Pressure and emotion

The strongest warning sign is pressure. Phishing messages create a reason to act immediately: your account will be closed today, your password expires in one hour, a payment failed, you have a fine to pay, a package is stuck. Some use fear (legal action, suspension, a security alert), others use reward (a refund, a prize, a scholarship). A legitimate organisation rarely demands an urgent action that can only be completed through a link in the message.

## Requests that should always stop you

Treat a message as suspicious whenever it asks you to:

- Enter your password on a page you reached through a link.
- Read out, type in or forward a one-time code or MFA code.
- Approve a sign-in notification you did not start yourself.
- Open an attachment you were not expecting, especially if it asks you to "enable content", "enable editing" or unzip a file with a password.
- Pay, buy gift cards, or change bank details.

## Sender and address clues

The name shown as the sender can be anything; only the actual address matters. Look for an address on a free email service claiming to be an organisation, a domain that is close to the real one but different (an extra letter, a hyphen, a swapped letter, a different ending such as .com instead of .ac.il), or a reply-to address that is different from the sender. Even when the sender address looks perfect, the message can still be fake, because attackers also send from accounts they have stolen.

## Link clues

Before clicking, look at where a link really goes. On a computer, hover over it; on a phone, press and hold. Warning signs include a domain you do not recognise, the real organisation's name appearing only at the start of a long address (for example college-name.login-check.example), a raw number address instead of a name, a link shortener that hides the destination, and letters that look normal but come from another alphabet. A padlock (HTTPS) only means the connection is encrypted; phishing sites use HTTPS too.

## Content clues

Generic greetings ("Dear user", "Dear student"), unusual spelling or grammar, logos that look slightly wrong, and messages that do not match how the organisation normally contacts you are all supporting signs. None of them proves anything alone, and well-made phishing may have none of them. When in doubt, verify the message through a channel you already trust instead of using anything inside the message.
